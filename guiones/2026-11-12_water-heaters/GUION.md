## 4 · Cómo funciona cada uno (1:40–3:00)
**Narración**
> Now, how each one works.
> A regular tank water heater keeps a big tank of water hot all day, whether you use it or not. A typical one holds around fifty gallons.
> A tankless water heater has no tank. It heats water only when you open a tap, using a powerful gas burner or electric element.
> A heat pump water heater looks like a regular tank, but it works like the heat pump we covered in the heating video. Instead of making heat directly, it pulls heat from the air around it and moves it into the water.
> To compare them fairly, there's one number that matters: the Uniform Energy Factor, or UEF. It comes from a standard Department of Energy test. The higher the UEF, the less energy the water heater needs for the same hot water.

**Imagen**

**Texto en pantalla:** `UEF: higher = less energy for the same hot water`

## 5 · Las eficiencias oficiales (3:00–4:10)
**Narración**
> So here are the official numbers.
> Under current federal rules, a standard fifty-gallon electric tank has to reach a UEF of about point nine two. In other words, almost all the electricity it uses ends up as heat in the water.
> An electric tankless heater has to reach about point nine one. That's basically the same.
> That's the key point. Electric resistance can't get much better than turning one unit of electricity into one unit of heat, whether there's a tank or not.
> A heat pump water heater is in another league. To earn the ENERGY STAR label, a standard heat pump water heater needs a UEF of at least three point three. That's about three point six times as efficient as the electric tank.
> On the gas side, a minimum fifty-gallon gas tank is rated around point five six. ENERGY STAR gas tanks need at least point eight one, and ENERGY STAR gas tankless units need at least point nine five.

**Imagen:** la regla de UEF con marcas que van apareciendo: **0.92** electric tank, **0.91** electric tankless (casi encima de la anterior), **3.30** heat pump (salto grande, en azul), y en la zona de gas **0.56** gas tank, **0.81** ENERGY STAR gas tank y **0.95** ENERGY STAR gas tankless. Fuentes: 10 CFR 430.32(d) y ENERGY STAR.
**Texto en pantalla:** `Heat pump water heater ≈ 3.6× an electric tank`

## 6 · Casa eléctrica (4:10–5:20)
**Narración**
> Now let's put dollars on it, starting with a home that heats water with electricity.
> The government's household survey says the average electric water heater uses about two thousand seven hundred kilowatt-hours a year. At today's average price, that's the four hundred ninety-two dollars from my ranking.
> Swap that tank for an electric tankless unit, and you'd spend about four hundred ninety-eight dollars a year. A few dollars more, not less.
> Swap it for an ENERGY STAR heat pump water heater, and it drops to about a hundred thirty-seven dollars a year.
> That's around three hundred fifty-five dollars saved every year, and a cut of about seventy percent.
> So for an electric home, the answer is clear. Tankless is not the efficiency upgrade. The heat pump water heater is.

## 7 · Casa de gas (6:10–7:30)
**Narración**
> Now a home that heats water with natural gas.
> The survey says the average gas water heater burns about eighteen million BTUs a year. With a minimum-efficiency gas tank at today's gas prices, that's about two hundred sixty-nine dollars a year.
> An ENERGY STAR gas tank would bring that down to about a hundred eighty-seven dollars.
> A tankless gas heater at the federal minimum lands at the same hundred eighty-seven. An ENERGY STAR tankless unit gets down to about a hundred fifty-nine dollars a year.
> And a heat pump water heater, running on electricity at today's average price, would cost about a hundred sixty-five dollars a year.
> So in a gas home, it's close. The best tankless unit and a heat pump water heater land within a few dollars of each other, and both save about a hundred dollars a year compared with a minimum gas tank.
> The big takeaway for a gas home: the biggest jump comes from leaving the minimum-efficiency tank behind, whichever upgrade you choose.

## 8 · La letra pequeña (7:30–8:50)
**Narración**
> Before you decide, there are a few practical limits.
> A tankless unit has to heat water as fast as you use it. ENERGY STAR gives typical flow rates: about two and a half gallons per minute for a shower, two point two for a sink, and three point three for a clothes washer.
> A certified gas tankless unit only has to deliver at least two point eight gallons per minute at a sixty-seven degree temperature rise.
> So a shower and a kitchen sink running at the same time can already ask for more than that. Bigger households often need a larger unit.
> A heat pump water heater has its own limits. ENERGY STAR says it needs to sit in a space that stays between forty and ninety degrees, with about a thousand cubic feet of air around it, like a basement, a garage or a utility room.
> It pulls heat out of that air, so the room around it gets a little cooler, which is a bonus in a hot garage and a drawback in a cold one.
> And one more data point: only about six and a half percent of American homes have a tankless water heater at all. Most homes still have a tank.

**Imagen**

- Fórmula: `2.5 + 2.2 = 4.7 GPM` frente a `2.8 GPM (ENERGY STAR minimum)`, en rojo.
- Casa en corte con foco `termo` en el sótano, rótulo «40–90°F · ~1,000 cubic feet of air».

**Texto en pantalla:** `Shower + sink = 4.7 GPM · Tankless minimum: 2.8 GPM`

## 9 · Cómo decidir (8:50–9:50) — la recompensa
**Narración**
> So here's how to decide.
> If you heat water with electricity and you have a basement, garage or utility room with enough space, a heat pump water heater is the clear winner on running costs. It cuts the bill by about seventy percent.
> If you heat water with electricity and you're thinking about tankless to save energy, the efficiency numbers say it won't do much. Its real benefits are space and endless hot water, not a lower bill.
> If you heat with gas, the first step is getting off a minimum-efficiency tank. An ENERGY STAR tank, an ENERGY STAR tankless unit, or a heat pump water heater all save real money.
> And whatever you have, the hot water itself is the cost. ENERGY STAR says about ninety percent of the energy it takes to run a clothes washer goes into heating the water.
> So washing clothes in cold water and taking shorter showers shrink the bill, no matter which machine heats the water.

**Imagen:** árbol de decisión con iconos: «Electric?» → «Space for it?» → **Heat pump water heater (−72%)**; «Electric + tankless?» → **≈ $0 saved**; «Gas?» → **leave the minimum tank (~−$80 to −$110)**. Al final, iconos de ropa en agua fría y de ducha corta.
