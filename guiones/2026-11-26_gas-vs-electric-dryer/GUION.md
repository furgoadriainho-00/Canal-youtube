## 3 · Quién tiene qué (0:45–1:35)
**Narración**
> First, who has what.
> According to the EIA's household energy survey, about one hundred two point three two million American homes have a clothes dryer.

> And how much do people use them? Most homes with a dryer, sixty-six percent, run one to four loads a week. About a quarter run five to nine.
> About twenty-one point two million homes have no dryer at all.

## 4 · Cómo funciona una secadora (1:35–2:30)
**Narración**
> Both types work the same way. A drum turns your clothes, a fan pulls air through, and something heats that air so the water in the clothes turns to vapor and gets blown out the vent.
> The only difference is what makes the heat.
> In an electric dryer, it's a heating element, basically a big electric heater.
> In a gas dryer, it's a small gas burner.
> A gas dryer still plugs into the wall, because the drum, the fan and the controls run on electricity. But the heat, which is most of the job, comes from gas.
> And that heat is where your money goes.

**Imagen:** diagrama con iconos sobre el clip `secadora.mp4`: tambor que gira, flechas de aire, ola de calor y gotas que salen por el tubo de ventilación. Dos versiones lado a lado: resistencia (rayo, naranja) y quemador (llama, azul); en la de gas, un enchufe pequeño con el rótulo «drum + fan + controls».
**Texto en pantalla:** `Same job · different heat`

## 5 · La energía que usan de verdad (2:30–3:45)
**Narración**
> So how much energy does a dryer use in a real home?
> The survey measured it. The average home with an electric dryer uses about two point three one nine million B T U of electricity a year for drying. That's about six hundred seventy-nine point six kilowatt-hours.
> The average home with a gas dryer uses about two point zero six four million B T U of natural gas.
> In other words, the gas dryer homes use about eighty-nine percent of the energy of the electric ones. Roughly the same job, roughly the same energy.
> One caveat: that gas number doesn't include the bit of electricity a gas dryer uses for its drum and fan. So the real gap is a little smaller than it looks.
> Either way, the energy is close. The money isn't.

## 6 · El precio de la misma energía (3:45–5:00)
**Narración**
> Here's the real reason. The same amount of energy costs very different amounts depending on how it reaches your house.
> At the national average electricity price of eighteen point one nine cents per kilowatt-hour, one million B T U of electricity costs about fifty-three dollars and thirty-one cents.
> At the national average residential gas price, one million B T U of natural gas costs about fourteen dollars and eighty-one cents.
> That's about three point six times as much for electricity, for the exact same amount of heat.
> And it changes a lot by state. In Florida, where gas is expensive, electricity is only about one point eight times the price of gas. In Illinois, where gas is cheap, it's about five point two times.
> So put it together: a similar amount of energy, times a very different price. The dryer itself doesn't decide your bill nearly as much as the rates on your utility bills do.

**Imagen**
- Dos montones de monedas para «1 million BTU»: **$53.31** (luz, naranja) frente a **$14.81** (gas, azul, CHEAPER). Fórmula **53.31 ÷ 14.81 ≈ 3.6×**.
- Mapa con FL e IL resaltados: **1.8×** y **5.2×**.
- Tarjetas: EIA Electric Power Monthly y EIA Natural Gas Prices.

**Texto en pantalla:** `Same heat: 3.6× the price`

## 8 · Lo que dicen las normas federales (6:40–7:40)
**Narración**
> What about efficiency? Every new dryer has to meet a federal rule, measured in pounds of clothes dried per kilowatt-hour of energy.
> In the government's test, a standard dryer dries an eight point four five pound load.
> Since twenty fifteen, a standard vented electric dryer must dry at least three point seven three pounds per kilowatt-hour. That's at most about two point two seven kilowatt-hours for the test load, or about forty-one cents at the national average price.
> For gas dryers, the minimum is three point three zero pounds per kilowatt-hour of energy, counting the gas as its kilowatt-hour equivalent.
> So on paper, a new electric dryer is a bit more efficient than a new gas one. But because gas energy is so much cheaper, gas still costs less to run.
> And for dryers made from March first, twenty twenty-eight, the minimums rise to three point nine three for electric, and three point four eight for gas. For electric, that's about five point one percent less energy per load. Helpful, but nowhere near the gap in prices.
> There's another detail in that same federal test. Until twenty twenty-eight, it assumes a typical home runs two hundred eighty-three dryer loads a year, about five point four a week. From March twenty twenty-eight, the test assumes two hundred thirty-six, about four point five a week.

**Imagen**
- Cesta de ropa en una báscula: **8.45 lb**. Fórmula **8.45 ÷ 3.73 ≈ 2.27 kWh** → **× 18.19¢ ≈ $0.41**.
- Regla de eficiencia con las marcas: **3.30** (gas, 2015), **3.48** (gas, 2028), **3.73** (eléctrica, 2015) y **3.93** (eléctrica, 2028). Anillo **5.1%**.
- Tarjeta: 10 CFR 430.32(h) y apéndice D2.

## 8b · ¿Y si tu secadora es vieja? (7:40–8:20)
**Narración**
> What if your dryer is old?
> In twenty twenty, about twenty-nine point six million American dryers were ten years old or more. That's twenty-nine percent of all dryers in homes.
> Those dryers were made under the older federal rule, from nineteen ninety-four, which required an electric standard dryer to dry at least three point zero one pounds per kilowatt-hour. On the test load, that's up to about two point eight one kilowatt-hours, compared with two point two seven for a dryer built to the twenty fifteen rule.
> The two rules measure efficiency in slightly different ways, so it's not a perfect comparison. But it tells you the floor was lower back then.
> So if your old electric dryer still works, replacing it with a new electric one won't change your bill nearly as much as the choice between gas and electric does. And if it's dying anyway, that's exactly the moment to look at your options.

**Imagen**
- Barras horizontales de edad de las secadoras (millones): **10–14 years 17.68M**, **15–19 years 7.03M**, **20+ years 4.89M** → cifra **29.6M** y anillo **29%**.
- Regla con las marcas **3.01** (1994) y **3.73** (2015); fórmula **8.45 ÷ 3.01 ≈ 2.81 kWh** frente a **2.27 kWh**.
- Tarjetas: EIA RECS 2020 HC3.1 · 10 CFR 430.32(h)(2) y (3).

**Texto en pantalla:** `29.6M dryers are 10+ years old`

## 9 · ¿Merece la pena cambiar? (8:20–9:20)
**Narración**
> So should you switch from electric to gas?
> Over ten years at today's national average prices, the average electric dryer would cost about one thousand two hundred thirty-six dollars to run, and the average gas dryer about three hundred six. That leaves out the gas dryer's small electricity use and any price changes.
> That difference is real. But a gas dryer needs a gas line and a vent where your laundry is. If your home already has gas at the dryer, gas is usually the cheaper choice to run.
> If it doesn't, the savings have to pay for that work first, and that depends on your home and your installer. Get a quote and compare it with your state's yearly difference.
> And if you're replacing an electric dryer with another electric one, the federal rules mean any new model you buy already has to meet the efficiency minimum.
> There's also a simpler lever: the number of loads. Every load you skip in a new electric dryer saves up to about forty-one cents at the national average price, about thirty-three cents in Washington, and about forty-four cents in Illinois.
> Fuller loads, a faster spin in the washer so clothes come out less wet, or a clothesline on a sunny day all mean fewer kilowatt-hours going out the vent.

**Imagen**
- Dos barras de 10 años: **$1,236** (eléctrica) frente a **$306** (gas, CHEAPER).

## 10 · Resumen y cierre (9:20–10:00)
**Narración**
> So here's the bottom line.
> Gas and electric dryers use a similar amount of energy. The difference is the price of that energy: across the US, electricity costs about three point six times as much as gas for the same heat.
> That's why the average electric dryer costs about one hundred twenty-four dollars a year to run, and the average gas dryer about thirty-one.
> In the places I checked, gas saves somewhere between about fifty-three and one hundred eight dollars a year.
> If you already have gas at the dryer, use it. If you don't, compare the install quote with those yearly savings before you decide. And either way, fewer, fuller loads cost less.
> And if you're curious how the water heater in the same room stacks up, I compared tank, tankless and heat pump water heaters right here on the channel.

**Imagen:** las dos barras del principio (**2.319** y **2.064** million BTU) que se transforman en **$124** y **$31**; cifras **3.6×** y **$53–$108/yr**. Pantalla final hacia «Tank vs Tankless vs Heat Pump Water Heater».
