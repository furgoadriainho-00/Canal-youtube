## 3 · Momento 1: 1879, un hilo de algodón (0:45–2:00)
**Narración**
> Moment one: October twenty-second, eighteen seventy-nine, Menlo Park, New Jersey.
> Thomas Edison's team had already spent about a year and a half testing materials for the glowing part of a lamp, the filament.
> The filament is the whole trick of an incandescent bulb. Electricity flows through it, it gets so hot that it glows, and the glass bulb keeps the air out so it doesn't simply burn up.
> That morning, they tried a piece of cotton thread, carbonized, which means baked in a sealed chamber until only carbon was left.
> They sealed it inside a glass bulb and turned it on. It kept glowing for thirteen and a half hours.
> The Edison Papers project at Rutgers University, which keeps the lab's records, calls it the first successful experiment with a high-resistance carbon filament.
> Then they pushed more power through it, the bulb overheated and cracked about an hour later. But for the first time, they had a lamp with real commercial promise.
> Ten days later, on November first, Edison filed a patent application for the carbon filament.
> And on December thirty-first, he held the first public demonstration of his electric lighting system at Menlo Park.

**Imagen**
- Línea de tiempo en **1879** (marca naranja). Icono de bombilla que se enciende poco a poco, con un hilo dentro.
- Fórmula con iconos: «cotton thread» + llama (horno) → «carbon». Reloj que corre hasta **13.5 hours**.
- Grieta en la bombilla (icono), y después tres marcas en un calendario: **Oct 22**, **Nov 1** (patent) y **Dec 31** (public demo).
- Tarjeta de fuente: Thomas A. Edison Papers, Rutgers.

## 4 · Momento 2: 1880–1882, bambú y la primera central (2:00–3:15)
**Narración**
> But a lamp is useless without electricity to run it, and in eighteen seventy-nine almost no home had any.
> So Edison's team kept improving the filament. In July eighteen eighty, they tested bamboo, and bamboo filaments became standard in Edison lamps.
> Moment two: September fourth, eighteen eighty-two.
> At two fifty-seven Pearl Street, in lower Manhattan, Edison's generating station began supplying electricity to paying customers.
> On the first day, there were about four hundred lamps, for about eighty-five customers.
> Within a year, there were about ten thousand lamps, serving five hundred thirteen customers.
> That's the moment the light bulb stopped being an experiment and became something you pay for every month. Which brings us to the bill.

**Imagen**

## 5 · Cómo se mide la luz (3:15–4:15)
**Narración**
> Before we jump forward, one thing you need to know to read a light bulb box.
> For most of the twentieth century, people bought bulbs by watts. But watts measure the energy a bulb uses, not how bright it is.
> Brightness is measured in lumens.
> A traditional sixty-watt incandescent bulb gives about eight hundred lumens. That's the brightness most of us picture as a normal bulb.
> So in this video, every bulb I compare gives the same eight hundred lumens. The only question is how many watts it takes to get there.
> And to put a price on it, I'll assume each bulb is on three hours a day, at the national average electricity price of eighteen point one nine cents per kilowatt-hour.
> Here's the math, so you can do it for any bulb in your house. Take the watts, multiply by the hours it's on in a year, and divide by one thousand. That gives you kilowatt-hours. Then multiply by your price.
> Three hours a day is one thousand ninety-five hours a year. So a sixty-watt bulb uses about sixty-five point seven kilowatt-hours a year.
> At that rate, the classic sixty-watt bulb costs about eleven dollars and ninety-five cents a year. Just one bulb.

**Imagen**
- Caja de bombilla (iconos) con dos etiquetas: «watts = energy» y «lumens = brightness».
- Regla de brillo con la marca **800 lumens** y la bombilla de **60 W** encima.
- Iconos: reloj **3 h/day** y contador de luz `medidor.mp4` con **18.19¢/kWh**.
- Cifra animada con monedas: **$11.95/yr**.
- Tarjeta de fuente: FTC.

**Texto en pantalla:** `60 W = about 800 lumens`

Fórmula animada (después de la cifra): **W × hours ÷ 1,000 = kWh** → **60 × 1,095 ÷ 1,000 = 65.7 kWh** → **× 18.19¢ = $11.95**.

## 6 · Momento 3: 2006, la espiral (4:15–5:00)
**Narración**
> Now jump forward more than a century. Light bulbs got cheaper and more common, but the basic idea, a hot glowing filament, stayed the same.
> Then came the compact fluorescent bulb, the spiral one.
> Moment three: January first, two thousand six. A federal standard required the common spiral bulbs of fifteen watts or more to give at least sixty lumens for every watt.
> By twenty fifteen, according to the government's household energy survey, thirty-two percent of American homes used fluorescent bulbs for all or most of their lighting.
> Thirty-one percent still used mostly incandescent or halogen bulbs. And LED? Just four percent.

**Imagen**

- Barras horizontales «Homes using mostly… (2015)»: CFL **32%**, incandescent/halogen **31%**, LED **4%** (azul, muy corta).

## 7 · Momento 4: 2012–2014, la bombilla clásica se encoge (5:00–5:45)
**Narración**
> Moment four: the old bulb gets a limit.
> Between twenty twelve and twenty fourteen, a federal standard capped how many watts a regular bulb could use for each level of brightness.
> It came in steps. The brightest bulbs went first: from January first, twenty twelve, a bulb giving fourteen hundred ninety to twenty-six hundred lumens could use no more than seventy-two watts. In twenty thirteen, the next size down was capped at fifty-three watts.
> For an eight-hundred-lumen bulb, the limit became forty-three watts, starting January first, twenty fourteen.
> So a new eight-hundred-lumen bulb on store shelves had to use forty-three watts or less.
> Running cost? About eight dollars and fifty-six cents a year, instead of eleven dollars and ninety-five cents. Better, but still almost five times what a typical LED uses for the same light.

**Imagen**
- Escalones en la línea de tiempo: **2012 · 1,490–2,600 lumens · max 72 W**, **2013 · max 53 W** y **2014 · 800 lumens · max 43 W**.
- Línea de tiempo en **2014**. Comparación de dos barras: **60 W** frente a **43 W** (CHEAPER), las dos con «800 lumens».
- Cifras **$11.95** → **$8.56** por año.
- Tarjeta: 10 CFR 430.32(dd)(1)(i).

**Texto en pantalla:** `800 lumens: max 43 W (2014)`

## 8 · Momento 5: 2020–2022, llega el LED (5:45–7:00)
**Narración**
> Moment five: the LED takes over.
> By twenty twenty, forty-seven percent of American homes used LED bulbs for all or most of their indoor lighting. That's about fifty-seven point seven million homes, up from four percent just five years earlier.
> Mostly fluorescent homes fell to twelve percent. Mostly incandescent or halogen homes, to fifteen percent.
> Then, starting July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-two, the federal government banned the sale of any general service bulb that gives less than forty-five lumens per watt.
> For an eight-hundred-lumen bulb, that's a limit of about seventeen point eight watts. The forty-three-watt bulb was out.
> And a typical LED does much better than the limit. The Federal Trade Commission says you can find an eight-hundred-lumen LED that uses only about nine watts.
> Nine watts, three hours a day, at the national average price: about one dollar and seventy-nine cents a year.
> And the shift kept going. By twenty twenty-four, the EIA says thirty-seven percent of households used LED bulbs for all of their indoor lighting.

**Imagen**
- Línea de tiempo en **2020**. Casa en corte con foco en `luz` y el clip `luz.mp4`.
- Barras horizontales «Homes using mostly… (2020)»: LED **47%** (azul, larga), incandescent/halogen **15%**, CFL **12%**. Cifra **57.7M homes**.
- Gráfico `serie` de LED **4%** (2015) → **47%** (2020); después, cifra **37%** «all LED» (2024).
- Línea de tiempo en **2022**: regla con la marca **45 lumens per watt** y **17.8 W** máximo para 800 lumens; la barra de **43 W** se tacha.
- Comparación de dos barras: **17.8 W** (límite) frente a **9 W** (LED típico, CHEAPER). Cifra **$1.79/yr**.
- Tarjetas: EIA RECS 2020 · 10 CFR 430.32(dd) · FTC · EIA (RECS 2024).

**Texto en pantalla:** `2020: 47% of homes mostly LED`

## 9 · Momento 6: 2028, el siguiente límite (7:00–7:40)
**Narración**
> Moment six hasn't happened yet.
> For general service bulbs made on or after July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-eight, the federal standard gets much tighter.
> For a regular eight-hundred-lumen LED bulb, the new formula works out to about one hundred twenty-four point three lumens per watt.
> That's a maximum of about six point four watts, and about one dollar and twenty-eight cents a year to run.
> The rule applies to bulbs made from that date on. Nobody comes to take the bulbs already in your lamps. It just changes what you'll find on the shelf.

**Imagen:** línea de tiempo en **2028** (marca punteada, «future»). Fórmula animada: **800 ÷ 124.3 ≈ 6.4 W**. Barra **$1.28/yr**. Tarjeta: 10 CFR 430.32(dd)(1)(iv).
**Texto en pantalla:** `2028: about 6.4 W for 800 lumens`

## 10 · Lo que vale en tu casa (7:40–8:50)
**Narración**
> So let's line up the whole story, one eight-hundred-lumen bulb, three hours a day, for a year.
> The classic sixty-watt incandescent: eleven dollars and ninety-five cents.
> The forty-three-watt bulb from twenty fourteen: eight dollars and fifty-six cents.
> The twenty twenty-two limit, seventeen point eight watts: three dollars and fifty-four cents.
> A nine-watt LED: one dollar and seventy-nine cents.
> And the twenty twenty-eight limit: one dollar and twenty-eight cents.
> Swap one sixty-watt bulb for a nine-watt LED, and you save about ten dollars and sixteen cents a year. Swap ten of them, and that's about one hundred two dollars a year.
> Where you live changes the number. In Washington State, where electricity averages fourteen point four two cents, that one swap saves about eight dollars and five cents a year. In California, at thirty-two point five eight cents, about eighteen dollars and nineteen cents.
> And there's still room to do that. In twenty twenty, about seventy-five point two million American homes still had at least one incandescent or halogen bulb. That's sixty-one percent of all homes.
> The biggest single win is often outside. About thirteen point six two million homes left at least one incandescent or halogen bulb on outdoors all night.
> If a sixty-watt porch light runs twelve hours a night, that's about forty-seven dollars and eighty cents a year. A nine-watt LED doing the same job: about seven dollars and seventeen cents. That's about forty dollars and sixty-three cents saved, from one bulb.

**Imagen**
- Ranking con cinco puestos (de más caro a más barato): **$11.95**, **$8.56**, **$3.54**, **$1.79** y **$1.28**, cada uno con su año en la línea de tiempo.
