## 3 · Mito 1: bajar la calefacción por la noche gasta más (0:40–1:55)
**Narración**
> Myth number one: turning your heat down at night or while you're away wastes energy, because the system has to work extra hard to warm the house back up.
> It sounds logical, and you've probably heard it before.
> Reality: the U.S. Department of Energy says the opposite. Setting your thermostat back for at least eight hours a day can save you as much as ten percent a year on heating and cooling costs.
> The logic is simple. A cooler house loses heat to the outdoors more slowly, so while it sits at the lower temperature, your system has less to replace.
> How much is ten percent? ENERGY STAR says the average American household spends more than nine hundred dollars a year on heating and cooling. Ten percent of that is up to about ninety dollars a year, for something your thermostat can do for free.
> If you'd rather not think about it, ENERGY STAR certified smart thermostats have to show, from real homes' data, that they cut heating run time by at least eight percent on average.
> And plenty of homes aren't doing it. In the government's twenty twenty household survey, about fifty point seven million homes kept their heat at one temperature most of the time.

**Imagen**

- Cifra **10%** con anillo; tarjeta del DOE.
- Fórmula **$900 × 10% = up to $90/yr**; tarjeta de ENERGY STAR.

## 4 · Mito 2: las bombas de calor no funcionan bajo cero (1:55–3:05)
**Narración**
> Myth number two: heat pumps stop working once it's below freezing.
> Reality: cold air still holds a lot of heat. Measured from absolute zero, which is minus four hundred fifty-nine point six seven degrees Fahrenheit, air at zero degrees still has about eighty-seven percent of the heat energy of air at seventy degrees.
> Even at thirty-two degrees, it's about ninety-three percent.
> A heat pump uses electricity to move that heat inside, instead of making heat from scratch.
> And it keeps doing it in the cold. ENERGY STAR's cold-climate heat pumps must still deliver at least one point seven five units of heat for every unit of electricity at five degrees Fahrenheit. An electric baseboard heater delivers one.
> In a typical Minnesota home, I worked out that a heat pump at the federal minimum costs about one thousand sixty-five dollars a year to heat with, versus about two thousand three hundred forty-two for electric baseboard heat.
> In Georgia, about seven hundred twenty-seven versus one thousand five hundred ninety-seven. The colder the place, the bigger the savings, which is the exact opposite of the myth.

**Imagen**

## 5 · Mito 3: el calentador sin depósito siempre ahorra (3:05–4:10)
**Narración**
> Myth number three: switching to a tankless water heater always saves money.
> Only about six point five percent of American homes have one, but it's one of the most talked-about upgrades.
> Reality: if your water heater runs on electricity, it barely changes anything. Using the federal efficiency ratings and the government's data on how much hot water homes use, a regular electric tank costs about four hundred ninety-two dollars a year to run. An electric tankless unit at the federal minimum, about four hundred ninety-eight.
> That's essentially the same. Both turn electricity into heat almost one to one.
> In a gas home, the myth is closer to true. A gas tank at the federal minimum costs about two hundred sixty-nine dollars a year, and a gas tankless unit at the minimum about one hundred eighty-seven. So with gas, tankless does save, about eighty-two dollars a year.
> The upgrade that really moves the number is a heat pump water heater. An ENERGY STAR model costs about one hundred thirty-seven dollars a year for the same hot water. That's about seventy-two percent less than the electric tank.

## 6 · Mito 4: un radiador portátil es la forma barata de calentar (4:10–5:15)
**Narración**
> Myth number four: a space heater is a cheap way to stay warm.
> Reality: it depends entirely on how long you run it. A fifteen-hundred-watt heater at full power uses one point five kilowatt-hours every hour. At the US average electricity price, that's about twenty-seven cents an hour.
> Eight hours a day for a month adds up to about sixty-five dollars. In Washington State, about fifty-two. In California, at the state's average price, about one hundred seventeen.
> And every unit of heat it gives you costs one unit of electricity. A heat pump at the federal minimum delivers the same heat for about forty-five percent of the electricity.
> So a space heater can make sense to warm one small room for a short time. As a main heat source, running for hours, it's one of the most expensive ways to heat with electricity.

## 7 · Mito 5: la lavadora gasta en hacer girar el tambor (5:15–6:15)
**Narración**
> Myth number five: most of a washing machine's energy goes into spinning the drum.
> Reality: ENERGY STAR says water heating consumes about ninety percent of the energy it takes to run a clothes washer. The motor is the small part.
> That means the temperature dial matters more than almost anything else. Switching from hot to warm can cut the energy use in half, according to ENERGY STAR, and cold cuts it even more.
> In twenty twenty, about eight point seven two million American homes still washed mostly in hot water. About fifty-three point one one million used warm. And about forty-two point one three million already used cold.

**Imagen**

## 8 · Mito 6: cambiar a LED no compensa (6:15–7:15)
**Narración**
> Myth number six: switching to LED bulbs isn't worth it, because lights barely use any electricity.
> Reality: one bulb is cheap, but the difference adds up. The Federal Trade Commission says a traditional sixty-watt incandescent gives about eight hundred lumens, and an LED can give the same eight hundred lumens with about nine watts.
> On three hours a day, at the national average price, the sixty-watt bulb costs about eleven dollars and ninety-five cents a year. The LED, about one dollar and seventy-nine cents.
> That's about ten dollars and sixteen cents a year for each bulb you swap. Ten of them, about one hundred two dollars.
> In fact, since July twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-two, the federal government has banned the sale of general service bulbs that give less than forty-five lumens per watt. The old sixty-watt bulb is already off the shelf.
> The biggest single swap is often the light that stays on longest. A sixty-watt porch light on twelve hours a night costs about forty-seven dollars and eighty cents a year. A nine-watt LED doing the same job, about seven dollars and seventeen cents.
> And there's still room. In twenty twenty, about seventy-five point two million homes had at least one incandescent or halogen bulb.

## 9 · Mito 7: los aparatos de gas son más baratos porque gastan menos energía (7:15–8:40)
**Narración**
> Myth number seven: gas appliances are cheaper to run because they use less energy.
> Reality: often they use about the same, or even more. Look at the government's household energy survey.
> The average gas stove used about twice the energy of the average electric stove. And yet the electric one cost about fifty-two dollars and eighty-three cents a year to run, and the gas one about twenty-nine dollars and ninety-five cents.
> Dryers tell the same story. The average electric dryer and the average gas dryer used a similar amount of energy. But the electric one cost about one hundred twenty-four dollars a year, and the gas one about thirty-one.
> The reason is the price per unit of energy. At US average prices, one million B T U of electricity costs about fifty-three dollars and thirty-one cents. The same energy as natural gas, about fourteen dollars and eighty-one cents.
> So gas is usually cheaper not because the appliance is more efficient, but because the energy is cheaper.
> That's also why a heat pump can beat a gas furnace in some states and lose in others. In Minnesota, where gas is cheap, a new gas furnace heats a typical home for about five hundred forty-two dollars a year, versus about one thousand sixty-five for a heat pump. In Florida, where gas is expensive, the heat pump wins, about three hundred forty versus four hundred thirty-one.
> It all depends on your local prices.

## 10 · Resumen y cierre (8:40–9:30)
**Narración**
> So here are the seven myths, and what the numbers say.
> Turning the heat down saves energy, up to about ten percent. Heat pumps keep working in the cold. Electric tankless barely saves anything, but a heat pump water heater does. Space heaters are expensive as a main heat source. Most washer energy heats water. LEDs save about ten dollars a year per bulb. And gas is usually cheaper because of its price, not because it uses less energy.
> If you only do two things this week, check your thermostat schedule, and turn your washer dial to cold. Neither one costs a cent, and together they cut into heating and hot water.
